Work

How healthcare practitioners in Islamabad and Rawalpindi see telemedicine

My undergraduate thesis: a cross-sectional survey of 120 practitioners on what they know about telemedicine, what they think of it, and whether they would use it.

Where
Bahria University, Islamabad
When
2024, defended before a faculty committee
Sample
120 practitioners, including PIMS and KRL Hospital
Supervisor
Dr. Sidra Shahid

The question

Telemedicine use grew during COVID-19, but in Pakistan it depends on whether practitioners understand it and want to use it. I asked three things: how much do doctors in Islamabad and Rawalpindi know about telemedicine, how do they perceive it, and how willing are they to adopt it?

Design

  • Study type: quantitative cross-sectional survey, purposive sampling.
  • Participants: 120 healthcare practitioners in the twin cities: 44 doctors, 31 residents, 7 physicians, and 38 specialists across obstetrics and gynecology, medicine, family medicine, pediatrics, dermatology, orthopedics, surgery, and other fields.
  • Instrument: a questionnaire I built around the Technology Acceptance Model (perceived usefulness and ease of use) and Diffusion of Innovation theory.
  • Scoring: knowledge, perception, and willingness scales, each dichotomized; respondents scoring 60% or more were classed as good, positive, or high.
  • Analysis: SPSS, descriptive statistics and frequency distributions.

What I found

Practitioners were open to telemedicine but didn't know much about it. 76.0% had a positive perception and 77.7% high willingness to adopt, while 75.2% scored as having poor knowledge.

  • 86.0% agreed telemedicine is a feasible way to deliver care, and 76.0% agreed it could be integrated into the existing health system.
  • The gap between attitude and knowledge points to training as the main lever: the barrier to adoption is not reluctance.

Recommendations

The findings support adding telemedicine to practitioner education and continuing training, and building policy for telemedicine infrastructure in Pakistan's health system.

Limitations

  • Purposive sampling in two cities limits how far the results generalize.
  • Self-reported, descriptive results; no inferential testing of predictors.

Defense

I defended the thesis before a faculty committee with invited external examiners from other universities. It has not been published.

Methods

  • Cross-sectional survey
  • Instrument design
  • Knowledge, perception, and willingness scales

Tools

  • SPSS
  • Technology Acceptance Model
  • Diffusion of Innovation

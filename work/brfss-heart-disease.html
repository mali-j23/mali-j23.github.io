Heart disease and its socioeconomic gradient among Connecticut adults

A survey-weighted analysis of the 2024 Connecticut BRFSS in SAS: how common reported heart disease is, and which behavioral and economic factors travel with it.

Where
Connecticut Department of Public Health
When
Spring internship, Feb to May 2026
Data
2024 Connecticut BRFSS, 7,213 adults
Output
Presented to CTDPH's Chronic Disease and Injury Prevention Section, Apr 27, 2026

The question

Heart disease is the leading cause of death in Connecticut. State screening and prevention programs need to know which adults carry the most burden. I looked at how prevalence and odds differ by sex, race and ethnicity, income, obesity, smoking, and access to a personal doctor.

Data and outcome

The 2024 Connecticut Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, a state telephone survey of adults (7,213 respondents). The outcome is a composite I built from three self-reported diagnoses: heart attack, coronary heart disease, or stroke. Respondents who answered "no" to all three were coded 0; anyone with a missing answer and no "yes" was set to missing.

What I did in SAS

  • Recoded raw BRFSS variables into analysis categories: income bands, race and ethnicity groups from _RACEGR3, obesity from _BMI5CAT, current smoking from _RFSMOK3, and personal doctor from PERSDOC3. Refused and don't-know answers were set to missing, except income, where they were kept as a separate category.
  • Estimated weighted prevalence with PROC SURVEYFREQ (Taylor series variance, strata _STSTR, clusters _PSU, weight _LLCPWT) and tested differences with the Rao-Scott chi-square.
  • Estimated unadjusted odds ratios with PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC under the same design. The script also specifies a full multivariable model; the results reported here are unadjusted.
/* Composite outcome: heart attack, coronary heart disease, or stroke */
array first [3] CVDINFR4 CVDCRHD4 CVDSTRK3;
array second [3] HeartAtt CHD Stroke;
do re = 1 to 3;
    if first[re] = 1 then second[re] = 1;
    else if first[re] = 2 then second[re] = 0;
end;

/* Survey-weighted prevalence with Rao-Scott chi-square */
proc surveyfreq data=brfss24ctv2 varmethod=taylor nomcar;
    strata _STSTR; cluster _psu; weight _LLCPWT;
    tables (sex RaceEth educat3cat obeseyn currentsmoker)*heartdis / row cl chisq;
run;

/* Odds ratio for one predictor, design-adjusted */
proc surveylogistic data=brfss24ctv2 nomcar;
    strata _ststr; cluster _psu; weight _LLCPWT;
    model heartdis(descending) = Inc25K Inc2549K Inc5074K IncMiss;
run;

Results

The weighted prevalence of reported heart disease among Connecticut adults was 8.75% (95% CI 7.88 to 9.62).

  • A clear income gradient. Odds rose as income fell: 2.19 for adults under $25K and 1.85 for $25K to $49,999, compared with $75K or more. The $50K to $74,999 group was borderline (CI 0.99 to 2.23).
  • Behavioral risk. Current smokers had 50% higher odds (OR 1.50) and adults with obesity 38% higher odds (OR 1.38).
  • Sex. Women had lower odds than men (OR 0.78); prevalence was 7.80% vs. 9.76%.
  • Race and ethnicity. Prevalence differences across groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.30).

Reading the personal-doctor result carefully. Adults with a personal doctor had 3.2 times the odds of reported heart disease. A doctor doesn't cause heart disease. People with regular care are more likely to be diagnosed and to report the diagnosis. That means BRFSS likely undercounts heart disease in adults without access, and the real income gap is probably wider than these numbers show.

Recommendations in my report

  • Expand targeted cardiovascular screening in high-poverty communities, focused on residents earning under $25K.
  • Design outreach for men through workplaces and community organizations.
  • Build smoking cessation and obesity management into routine care for Medicaid and low-income populations.
  • Use mobile health units for screening in underserved areas, to find cases that surveillance currently misses.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional and self-reported: these are associations, not causes, and diagnoses depend on access to care.
  • Odds ratios shown are unadjusted, so they don't account for confounding between income, smoking, obesity, and access.
  • The composite outcome combines heart attack, coronary heart disease, and stroke, which have different risk profiles.

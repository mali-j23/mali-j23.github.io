Heart disease and its income gradient among Connecticut adults

A survey-weighted analysis of the 2024 Connecticut BRFSS in SAS: how common reported heart disease is, and which groups carry more of it.

Study summary

Question
How common is reported heart disease among Connecticut adults, and how does it vary by sex, race and ethnicity, income, smoking, obesity, and access to a personal doctor?
Why it matters
Heart disease is the leading cause of death in Connecticut. Screening and prevention programs need to know which adults carry the most burden.
Data and population
2024 Connecticut Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, a statewide telephone survey of adults; 7,213 respondents. Outcome: ever told of a heart attack, coronary heart disease, or stroke (a composite I built from three BRFSS items).
My role
Spring 2026 internship project with guidance from Stephanie Poulin. I recoded the variables, wrote and ran the SAS analysis, interpreted the results, presented them to CTDPH's Chronic Disease and Injury Prevention Section, and wrote the report.
Methods
Weighted prevalence with PROC SURVEYFREQ (Taylor series variance, Rao-Scott chi-square). Unadjusted odds ratios with PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC. Both use the BRFSS strata, clusters and final weights.
Main finding
Weighted prevalence was 8.75% (95% CI 7.88–9.62). Adults earning under $25K had about twice the odds of reported heart disease compared with those earning $75K or more (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.56–3.06).
What it may mean
Burden follows income, smoking and obesity. The personal-doctor result (OR 3.23) most likely reflects diagnosis, which would mean the survey undercounts heart disease among adults without regular care.
Limitations
Cross-sectional and self-reported, so these are associations, not causes. Odds ratios are unadjusted. The composite outcome combines conditions with different risk profiles.

Odds ratios

Reading the results

  • An income gradient. Odds rose as income fell: 1.49 for $50K–$74,999 (CI includes 1), 1.85 for $25K–$49,999, and 2.19 under $25K, each compared with $75K or more.
  • Behavioral risk. Current smokers had 50% higher odds (OR 1.50) and adults with obesity 38% higher odds (OR 1.38).
  • Sex and race. Women had lower odds than men (OR 0.78). Differences across racial and ethnic groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.30).

The personal-doctor result. Adults with a personal doctor had 3.2 times the odds of reporting heart disease. A doctor doesn't cause heart disease; people with regular care are more likely to be diagnosed and to report it. If that's right, the true income gap is probably wider than these numbers show.

Recommendations in my report

Based on these associations, my report suggested that CTDPH:

  • expand targeted cardiovascular screening in high-poverty communities, focused on residents earning under $25K;
  • design outreach for men through workplaces and community organizations;
  • build smoking cessation and obesity management into routine care for Medicaid and low-income populations;
  • use mobile health units for screening in underserved areas, to reach cases surveillance currently misses.

Code

/* Composite outcome: heart attack, coronary heart disease, or stroke */
array first [3] CVDINFR4 CVDCRHD4 CVDSTRK3;
array second [3] HeartAtt CHD Stroke;
do re = 1 to 3;
    if first[re] = 1 then second[re] = 1;
    else if first[re] = 2 then second[re] = 0;
end;

/* Survey-weighted prevalence with Rao-Scott chi-square */
proc surveyfreq data=brfss24ctv2 varmethod=taylor nomcar;
    strata _STSTR; cluster _psu; weight _LLCPWT;
    tables (sex RaceEth educat3cat obeseyn currentsmoker)*heartdis / row cl chisq;
run;

/* Odds ratio for one predictor, design-adjusted */
proc surveylogistic data=brfss24ctv2 nomcar;
    strata _ststr; cluster _psu; weight _LLCPWT;
    model heartdis(descending) = Inc25K Inc2549K Inc5074K IncMiss;
run;
